ZERO‑L

A storm umbrella that makes no mean lift in level wind from any direction, has a frame that cannot turn inside out, and still covers you from rain and sun.

Run the storm simulatorWatch ZERO-L and a normal umbrella face the same monsoon squall in real time

Summary

An ordinary umbrella is a thin, deeply cambered wing with an open cup underneath. It makes lift in a crosswind even when you hold it perfectly level, and the cup and the free rib tips let a gust turn it inside out. ZERO-L does not try to out-muscle the wind with a stronger frame. It removes each cause of the lift: the section is symmetric, the cup is closed, the rib tips are tied down, and one-way vents bleed off upward gusts.

Why a normal umbrella lifts

  • It is a cambered wing

    Cut a canopy through the shaft and you get a thin arc, curved like a heavily cambered aerofoil. Thin-aerofoil theory puts the zero-lift angle of a parabolic camber line at αL0 ≈ −2f/c. A typical canopy is 19 cm deep on 105 cm, so f/c ≈ 0.18 and αL0 ≈ −21°. Held level, it already sits 21° above its zero-lift angle and makes CL ≈ 0.7. That is 86 N of lift at 15 m/s. Tilting it about 21° into the wind cancels the lift, which is exactly what people learn to do by instinct.

  • Its underside is an open cup

    Any upward gust blows straight into the concave underside. A cup facing the flow has a drag coefficient near 1.35, more than a flat disc, and the trapped air pressurises the canopy from below.

  • Its rib tips are cantilevers

    The stretcher meets each rib at mid-span, so the outer half of every rib is a free cantilever. Pressure from below bends the tips up until the shell snaps through and the umbrella inverts. A typical stick umbrella does this at roughly 60 N of uplift, which the model reaches at 12.5 m/s in steady wind and 10.7 m/s with ±10° gusts.

The design

Each feature removes one of those causes. The numbers below match the callouts in Figure 2. The next section shows the moving parts at work.

  1. 1

    Symmetric double-wedge canopy

    The top and bottom skins are mirror images: 10° faces, 185 mm deep at the centre, a sharp rim. A section with no camber makes no lift when held level in level wind; that follows from symmetry alone. The canopy is also round, so this holds for wind from any compass direction and you never have to turn it to face the wind. Edge-on it is a thin double wedge, the classic supersonic aerofoil section, so its drag coefficient falls to about 0.05 against about 0.4 for a dome.

  2. 2

    Closed lower skin

    The cup is gone. There is no concave face for a gust to fill and nothing that behaves like a parachute.

  3. 3

    Tied-down rib tips

    A 2 mm Dyneema stay runs from each rib tip to the runner, inside the lower skin. Hub, tip and runner form a triangle, so each rib carries only axial load and its tip cannot rise without stretching the stay (Detail B). A severe 100 N uplift puts about 36 N in each stay, against a breaking load near 3 kN. Inversion is blocked by geometry, not resisted by stiffness. To close the umbrella you slide the runner down as usual; the stays go slack and the frame folds like any stick umbrella.

  4. 4

    One-way gust valves

    Sixteen shingled flaps in the top skin, with matching inward-opening flaps in the lower skin, open when pressure from below exceeds 15 Pa (Detail A). Closed, they lap like roof tiles and stay rain-tight. Open, air flows up and out of the slot, so rain is blown away from it. They clip upward gust spikes and leave downward ones alone on purpose: ZERO-L can push down into your hand, but it cannot pull out of it.

  5. 5

    Drip control

    The knife-edge rim (20° included angle) makes water leave cleanly instead of creeping round onto the lower skin. A drip ring on the shaft, borrowed from kayak paddles, catches what runs down before it reaches your hand. Both skins carry a fluorine-free water-repellent finish.

  6. 6

    Double-skin sun shield

    The top skin is a dense UPF 50+ weave with a reflective coating (solar reflectance of 0.8 or more). The 185 mm air gap works as a radiant barrier, like the double roof of a tent: the lower skin stays close to air temperature, and its matte charcoal face soaks up glare reflected from the pavement instead of bouncing it onto your face.

How it works in the real world

Two moving parts make ZERO-L behave differently in your hand. The frame locks into rigid triangles when you open it, and the valve flaps breathe when a gust hits. Drive both below, then follow one walk through a storm.

A walk through a storm

  1. Open it

    Push the runner up the shaft. The ribs swing out like any umbrella. The last centimetre feels firmer as the stays come tight, then the latch clicks and holds about 40 N in each stay.

  2. Light rain, still air

    It works like any umbrella. Water runs down the 10° faces and leaves at the knife-edge rim, clear of your shoulders. The closed valve flaps lap like roof tiles.

  3. The wind rises

    Keep it level. With no camber there is no steady lift to fight, and the sideways push is small: about 6 N at 15 m/s, where a normal umbrella pushes 47 N and pulls up 86 N.

  4. A gust from below

    The valves flick open for a fraction of a second and dump the overpressure. The upward tug stays small, about 21 N at 15 m/s with ±10° gusts against 118 N for a normal umbrella. The tied-down rib tips cannot flip.

  5. A gust from above

    The flaps are pressed onto their seats, so no rain is driven through. The canopy pushes down into your hand for a moment, up to about 40 N in the same conditions. It cannot be torn upward out of your grip.

  6. Rain starts driving sideways

    Tip it about 10° into the wind. That covers your head and shoulders and turns the force into a steady downward push, which is far easier to hold than an upward yank.

  7. Strong sun

    The reflective top sends most of the sunlight back, and the air gap keeps the underside near air temperature. Hold it a little lower so the shade reaches your shoulders.

  8. Close it and dry it

    Release the latch and slide the runner down. The stays go slack and it folds like a normal umbrella. Dry it open: two skins hold water longer than one, and the valves let air through the gap.

Build a prototype

The first working ZERO-L can be built on the frame of an ordinary 8-rib stick umbrella. The shaft, hub, runner and stretchers are reused; the ribs, skins, stays and valves are new.

  1. Strip the donor. Remove the canopy and ribs. Keep the shaft, hub, runner, stretchers and their pins.
  2. Fit the ribs. Pin the eight 533 mm carbon tubes to the hub, and pin each stretcher to its rib 266 mm from the hub.
  3. Move the latch. The runner must lock 185 mm below the hub. Drill a new latch hole there or fit a clip-on stop. This sets the ribs at 10° below horizontal.
  4. Fit the tip caps. Each printed cap holds a rib end and has an eye for the stay and slots for the two skin edges.
  5. Rig and tune the stays. Tie Dyneema from each tip-cap eye to a line-lock on the runner. Open and latch the frame, then tighten each stay until it rings at about 120 Hz when plucked; a phone tuner app reads this. Equal pitch means equal tension, about 40 N.
  6. Cut the skins. Each skin is 8 flat gores: isosceles triangles with 533 mm sides, a 402 mm base and a 44.3° apex angle, plus 10 mm seam allowance. Strictly this makes an octagonal double pyramid; top-to-bottom mirror symmetry is what matters, and it still holds.
  7. Cut the valves. In every gore of both skins, cut two 65 × 100 mm slots between 40% and 60% of the radius. Tape a PET flap along its hub-side edge so it laps past the slot on all sides, and set a magnet pair at its free edge. Top flaps lie outside and open outward; lower flaps lie inside and open inward.
  8. Sew and close. Sew the top skin to sleeves along the ribs and the lower skin to sleeves along the stays. Bind both edges together at the tip caps to form the knife-edge rim.
  9. Finish and check. Fit the drip ring just above the grip and spray both skins. Hold a hair dryer on low under each valve: both flaps should lift, then drop shut when you take it away.

What it cannot do

  • Stop sideways rain

    At 15 m/s rain arrives 67° from vertical. No overhead canopy covers more than your head and shoulders then. Tipping it into the rain buys cover, but any surface angled to the wind makes lift: 10° into a 15 m/s wind gives ZERO-L about 40 N of downforce. The next add-on to test is a porous storm skirt, a louvered band hung from the rim. Raindrops are far too heavy to follow air round a chevron path (Stokes number in the thousands), so the skirt catches them while air passes through, and a vertical band makes almost no vertical force in level wind.

  • Ignore vertical gusts

    Air moving up or down through the canopy changes its angle of attack, and any surface that blocks air feels that. ZERO-L's mean lift is zero and its upward spikes are clipped, but downward pushes of about 40 N remain at 15 m/s with ±10° gusts. Two-way rain-proof valves, built like the Dorade boxes that ventilate yachts in heavy weather, would clip those too. That is phase 2.

  • Stay perfectly symmetric in use

    Your head and shoulders under the canopy, and the wind shear near the ground, break the mirror symmetry slightly. The leftover lift has to be measured; the depth of the lower skin is the trim adjustment.

  • Make a storm walkable

    Above Beaufort 9 (about 21 m/s) people struggle to stay on their feet. ZERO-L is built to stay controllable to 20 m/s and to come through 35 m/s undamaged. It is a storm umbrella, not a hurricane tool.

  • Replace testing

    Every number in this note is a first-order estimate. The Method section lists the assumptions, and the test plan below is how to replace them with measurements.

Test plan

  1. Mock-up. Convert a standard 8-rib, 105 cm stick umbrella: flatten the canopy to 10° faces, add the lower skin and Dyneema stays, and set about 40 N of stay pre-tension at the runner lock. No valves yet.
  2. Force balance. Mount it on a six-component balance, or on a car-roof rig driven at 5 to 25 m/s on a still day. Sweep the angle of attack from −30° to +30° and record CL, CD and Cm. Pass: |CL| below 0.05 at 0°. The sharp rim fixes where the flow separates, so results carry across Reynolds numbers (full scale is about 1×10⁶ at 15 m/s) and a 1:3 model at 30 m/s is acceptable.
  3. Valves. Add the 16 + 16 flaps, then measure their opening pressure and the uplift curve. Target: upward pull of 25 N or less at 15 m/s and +10°.
  4. Inversion attempt. Pitch the canopy to +45° at 25 m/s. Pass: no permanent deformation.
  5. Rain and sun. Spray 50 mm/h from a sprinkler rig in front of a fan and weigh the water reaching a head-height target. Test the top skin's UV transmission (UPF, AS/NZS 4399) and log underside temperature in full sun.
  6. Field trial. Walk it in real weather with a handheld anemometer and a load cell in the grip.

Method

Forces come from a quasi-steady normal-force model for a low-aspect-ratio plate: an attached-flow term plus a crossflow term, resolved into lift and drag. It is deliberately simple, so every assumption can be checked against the wind tunnel.

F = C·q·S q = ½ρU², ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, S = 0.866 m² C_N = C_Lα·sin α_e·cos α_e + C_Dc·sin α_e·|sin α_e| C_Lα = 1.83 /rad C_L = C_N·cos α_e C_D = C_D0 + C_N·sin α_e Conventional: α_e = α + 20.6°, C_Dc = 1.35 into the cup, 0.8 onto the dome, C_D0 = 0.12, |C_N| ≤ 1.45 ZERO-L: α_e = α, C_Dc = 1.0, C_D0 = 0.05, |C_N| ≤ 1.3 upward normal force above the 13 N valve load is cut to 30 % (assumed vent effectiveness 70 %)

Gusts swing the angle of attack by ±γ and the peaks are taken quasi-steadily, which overstates the effect of very short gusts. The 60 N inversion load for a stick umbrella is an assumed typical value. Raindrops are 2 mm, falling at 6.5 m/s (Gunn and Kinzer, 1949). The wind lab runs this same model in your browser.
