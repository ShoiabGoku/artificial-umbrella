What the simulation found

The design note's static model missed one thing. A flat canopy's gust lift acts about a quarter of its diameter upwind of the shaft, so every gust also twists the umbrella in your hand. With one-way valves, downward gusts are not relieved, and in strong gusty wind they can tip ZERO-L into the wind faster than a hand reacts. Two-way valves fix most of this.

What is simulated

  • Wind. Mean wind plus turbulence as two random processes (along-street gusts with a 60 m length scale, vertical gusts with 4 m), carried down the street at the mean speed, so you can watch a gust travel through the rain before it reaches the umbrellas. Vertical gust intensity is 45% of the along-street value.
  • Aerodynamics. The same model as the design note, driven by the air speed relative to the moving canopy. Gust lift acts 0.25 m upwind of the centre and builds up over 1.5 canopy lengths of travel. ZERO-L's valves open in 40 ms and close in 80 ms.
  • The person. The arm is a 900 N/m spring. The hand resists tilt with muscle stiffness, plus a slower deliberate correction after a 150 ms reaction delay, up to 26 N·m with one hand or 50 N·m with two. A normal umbrella inverts at 60 N of uplift; either umbrella is lost above 170 N (one hand) or 280 N (two).
  • Rain. Drop sizes follow the Marshall–Palmer distribution and fall at their terminal speed; each drop takes about vt/g to catch up with a gust. Each drop carries water in proportion to its diameter cubed, so the large, steeper-falling drops carry most of it. Water reaching the head and shoulders is counted through a slab 0.45 m deep.
  • Not simulated: 3D flow, fabric flutter (drawn, not computed), the body sheltering the air under the canopy, and dynamic stall. Treat the numbers as first-order.
